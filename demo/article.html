Reading With a Pencil

Why the fastest way to remember what you read is to slow down, argue with the page and leave yourself a trail of notes.

By the editors · 9 min read · Updated this week

Most of what we read disappears within a few days. The words were understood, the argument made sense, and yet a week later only a vague impression remains. Readers who keep a pencil in hand tend to remember more, and not because they are copying the text. They are having a conversation with it, and a conversation is much harder to forget than a lecture.

Why the margin matters

The margin is the only part of a page that belongs to the reader. Printing leaves it empty on purpose, and generations of students have filled it with question marks, arrows, short summaries and the occasional impatient "no!". Each of those marks is a small decision: this sentence matters, this one is doubtful, this one connects to something I read last month.

Deciding is the useful part. When you choose what to mark, you are forced to judge the text instead of absorbing it. That judgement is what turns reading into learning. A page with nothing marked is usually a page that was skimmed, however slowly the eyes moved across it.

A highlight says "this matters". A note says why — and the why is what you will need when you come back.

The forgetting curve, briefly

A well-known memory experiment from the nineteenth century measured how quickly lists of nonsense syllables fade. The result has been repeated many times since: memories drop steeply in the first day and then level off. Reviewing material at growing intervals flattens the curve, and each review takes less effort than the one before.

Notes make those reviews cheap. Rereading a whole chapter is a chore; rereading six highlighted sentences and two margin notes takes a minute. The trick is to write notes you will understand later, which in practice means one idea per note, written in your own words.

Four colors, four jobs

Color works best when every color has a single, predictable meaning. Many careful readers settle on something close to this scheme:

  • Yellow for the main idea of a section.
  • Green for evidence, data and examples.
  • Blue for definitions and new vocabulary.
  • Pink for questions and disagreements.

The exact colors do not matter. What matters is that a glance at a page tells you where the claims, the proof and the open questions are, without reading a single word again.

A routine that fits in ten minutes

  1. Skim the headings and the last paragraph before you start.
  2. Read one section at a time, highlighting at most one sentence in five.
  3. At the end of each section, write a two-line note in your own words.
  4. The next day, reread only your highlights and notes.
Reading habits compared
Habit Extra time What it buys you
Highlighting sparingly About 5% A page you can review in seconds
Two-line section notes About 10% The argument in your own words
Next-day review One or two minutes Most of the detail, weeks later

Notes that talk back

The best notes are slightly argumentative. "Interesting" is not a note; "this contradicts the chapter on habits — check the sample size" is. Notes like that turn a finished book into an open question, and open questions are what bring you back to a text.

Keep them short. A note that needs scrolling is a draft of an essay, which is a fine thing to write, but somewhere else. Here too, one idea per note keeps the margin readable.

Reading with a pencil on a screen

Screens took the pencil away. Selecting text and copying it into a separate document breaks the conversation: the note ends up far from the sentence that prompted it. Tools that let you mark the page itself, and bring those marks back when you return, restore the margin to where it belongs.

Whatever tool you use, the habit is the same: slow down, decide what matters, and leave a trail your future self can follow.
